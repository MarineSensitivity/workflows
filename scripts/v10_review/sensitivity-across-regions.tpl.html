Can a Program Area score be compared across regions?

Not today. Every metric is rescaled to 0–100 inside its BOEM Ecoregion, so each ecoregion is graded on its own curve and gets a 100 whether its best cell holds a nesting beach or a single passing leatherback. A national table built from those numbers compares curves, not turtles. This page shows what the curve does, what the proposed v10 distribution-share score does instead, what changes on the real Program Areas, and what the literature says about both.

Naming. Throughout this page v9 is the published rule (cell score → ecoregional rescale → zone mean) and v10 is the rule this page defines: each species' risk-weighted share of its global range, summed per component, expressed per km² against the national average concentration (100), with equal component weights and a coverage floor. Both are applied to the same v9 merged models: v10 is a scoring change, not new data.

Basis of this review

The method brief Distribution-Share Sensitivity Score for National Program Area Comparison (T. White, BOEM, 2026-09-03) proposes a nationally comparable score per species category, referred to here as v10. This page tests that proposal against the method as documented for the v9 pre-release, the Scoring and Model Merging chapters (review host, sign-in required), and cites the published formulas by their v7 numbers: Formula 9.1 (cell score), 9.4 (ecoregional rescale), 9.5 (zone aggregation) and 8.2 (turtle merge).

1 · The grading curve

The same 100 stands on very different ground

Category

Bars: the highest raw cell score (Formula 9.1) in each ecoregion that contains a 2026 Program Area, from v9 cell_metric. Press the button to see what Formula 9.4 does to every one of them. Hover a bar for the 99th percentile and the Program Areas inside. Ecoregion codes match the maps in the apps.

The v9 score is built in three moves. Each cell sums its species, weighted by extinction risk (Formula 9.1). Each ecoregion then stretches its own cells onto 0–100 (Formula 9.4). Program Areas average their cells (Formula 9.5).

The second move is where comparability is lost. The ecoregional rescale is deliberate and well argued in the Scoring chapter: without it, species-rich waters would always win. But it also guarantees that every ecoregion has a cell scoring exactly 100, so a Program Area's number says how close it sits to its own region's best, and nothing about how that best compares with anyone else's.

For sea turtles the raw ceilings differ by a factor of three between the East Bering Sea and the Eastern Gulf of America, and the Bering Sea's ceiling is set by a leatherback suitability surface in water where turtles are recorded a few times a decade. After rescaling, those ceilings are indistinguishable.

The docs already say scores "should not be compared across ecoregions". Table 10-2 of the first 11th-Program analysis does exactly that, which is the disagreement the brief sets out to resolve.

One change: normalize each species to itself, then rescale nationally

Each species sums to 1 over its global range (1), so a cell's value is the fraction of the species that lives there; cells sum their species, risk-weighted (2); zones sum their cells (3). The score (4) is that total as a fraction of the nation's, divided by the zone's fraction of the nation's surface: a concentration relative to the national average, 100 = a fair share per km², 200 = twice it. It is area-independent, the same reference for every area and every component, and stable across releases. The companion (5) is the total share of the world's importance, the "amount at stake". A component is reportable in a zone only if its cells cover ≥ 5 % of it (section 6); the composite is the equal-weight mean of the reportable components (section 4). With v binary and w = 1, (1)–(3) is rarity-weighted richness (Williams et al. 1996) and weighted endemism (Crisp et al. 2001), the per-feature normalization Zonation applies to every layer (Moilanen et al. 2005); (4) is a location quotient (Isserman 1977).

Reads as: a zone holding an entire endemic population scores far above 100; a zone holding a few vagrants scores near 0; a species-rich zone scores high only if its many species depend on it (section 5); no zone's score depends on any other zone's.

Why the score divides twice. The zone total (3) is not a percentage on its own: the fractions of a category sum to Σs ws/100, not to 1, and Program Areas do not tile the study area, so (4) first divides by the nation's total, shareUSA,g = Σs∈g ws · (fraction of the species' global range inside US waters)/100. And (3) sums over cells, so it grows with the size of the zone (section 9, finding 3); dividing by the zone's share of the nation's surface removes that, which is what makes the score a sensitivity, a property of place, rather than an inventory.

3 · Reference points

What a 100 means under v10

Under v10, 100 is the national average concentration of the component, and it is the same reference for every Program Area and every component. The score is a location quotient: the area's fraction of the nation's risk-weighted importance for the component, divided by the area's fraction of the nation's surface. A 100 means the area holds exactly its fair share per km²; 300 means three times the national concentration; 20 means a fifth of it. No area's score depends on any other area's, no component has a built-in 100, and the composite (the equal-weight mean of the reportable components) reads on the same scale: 100 = an area that is nationally average across the board. On v9's models the turtle score runs from –; the highest composite is –.

Two things the reference point deliberately is not. It is not area-dependent: a total share grows with the size of an area, so v10 divides it out and reports the total separately as the percent of the world's importance at stake (the companion column in every table). And it is not a best-performer reference: v9's 100 is the best cell of each component within each ecoregion (twelve incomparable reference points per component), and a "top Program Area = 100" stretch would have made one area per component the 100 and tied every score to whichever area happened to be first in that release. In the Ocean Health Index typology those are spatial comparisons; a fixed reference is preferred when one exists (Halpern et al. 2012; Samhouri et al. 2012), the composite-indicator literature calls it "distance to a reference" normalization and prefers it to min–max for the same reason (OECD/JRC 2008), and BOEM's OFWESA moved from RESA's comparison of study areas to fixed hypothetical maxima so that "the results would be independent of the sensitivity of other regions" (Morandi et al. 2018). The national average is the fixed reference that exists in the data itself; a policy bar such as the 1 % of a global population that Ramsar and the KBA standard use for a site of importance is the natural reference for the companion total (IUCN 2016; Ramsar Criterion 6).

4 · Component weights

How much of the composite each component should carry

The composite is a weighted mean of the component scores present in an area, compositez = Σg Wg scorez,g / Σg Wg. v9 uses equal weights, which the flower plot draws as equal petal widths; so does v10, and this section is the sensitivity analysis behind that choice. v10 changes the petal lengths, not the widths, so the weighting question is the same one the Ocean Health Index faced: the weights are a statement of values, not a measurement, and the honest treatment is a stated default plus a sensitivity analysis under alternative value sets (Halpern et al. 2012 showed the index under "extractive" and "preservationist" weightings; Burgass et al. 2017 on weighting as the dominant structural uncertainty of such indices; OECD/JRC 2008 on weights as trade-off coefficients that must follow the framework, not the data).

Three candidate bases, and a dial between them: equal (each component is one voice); species count ng, the Living Planet Index's diversity weighting, which gives a taxonomic group weight in proportion to how much of biodiversity it is (McRae et al. 2017); and total risk weight Σs∈g ws, the component's extinction-risk-weighted importance, which favours the groups with the most listed species. The dial sets Wg = (baseg / geometric mean of the bases)γ, so γ = 0 is equal weighting and γ = 1 the full base; primary productivity has no species and keeps weight 1 throughout. Custom sliders stand in for stakeholder weight sets.

What each basis does on v9

  • Species count hands – of the composite to invertebrates and fishes together and – to sea turtles; it re-imports the richness skew that per-species normalization was introduced to control, and it counts richness twice, once inside each component (section 5) and again between them.
  • Total risk weight hands the composite to the components with the most listed species (fishes and invertebrates by count of Σw, then corals) and nearly erases primary producers (Σw 3.3); it turns the composite into an index of listed-species exposure, a different product from sensitivity.
  • Equal keeps every component one voice, which is what the flower plot has always said and what OFWESA did after averaging within groups (Morandi et al. 2018); Zonation practice likewise normalizes group weights so that a species-rich group cannot dominate (Lehtomäki & Moilanen 2013).

Recommendation

Publish equal weights and report the sensitivity. Equal weighting is the defensible default when the framework gives no basis for another (OECD/JRC 2008), it is the Ocean Health Index convention the flower plot borrows (Halpern et al. 2012), and both data-driven alternatives fail on their own terms here: species count double-counts richness, and total risk weight changes what the composite measures. Report, in the re-issued table's appendix, the composite and its ranks under γ = 1 for both bases and under one or two stakeholder sets (a regulatory emphasis on listed groups, a biodiversity emphasis on species-rich groups), with the Spearman against equal weights, exactly as OHI reported extractive versus preservationist perspectives. The dial above is that sensitivity analysis.

5 · Richness

Does v10 still favour species-rich waters?

Partly, and it should be said plainly. The three rules treat richness differently. The v9 ecoregional rescale hides it by construction: every ecoregion gets a 100, rich or poor. A national min–max of the raw score (the green dots in section 8) lets it dominate: under Formula 9.1 a wide-ranging species adds its suitability to every cell it occupies, so a region full of wide-ranging species piles up score in every one of its cells. v10 sits between the two. Each species is counted once, split across its range, so it can no longer add to every region at the same time; but a zone's share is still a sum over the species present in it. Written out, the zone total is a product:

identitysharez,g = nz,g × means present( ws · shares,z / 100 )

richness × mean dependence. A species-rich zone outscores a species-poor one only if its many species depend on it enough: 1,000 tropical fishes each with 1 % of their range in a Gulf area weigh exactly as much as 20 Arctic fishes each with 50 % of theirs in a Bering area. Whether that is the right balance is the policy question the docs already name ("the fewer species an ecosystem has, the more each one matters"). v10 answers it with the species' own fractions rather than with a regional curve, and it publishes both factors, so a reader can see whether an area's score comes from how many species reach it or from how much of each species lives there.

If richness should count less than v10 lets it

  • Mean share per species (BOEM's OFWESA convention; Crisp's corrected weighted endemism) removes n entirely, and with it the difference between one endemic and thirty: a zone with a single endemic scores as high as one with ten. Shown as the fifth rule in the sandbox.
  • Core-area rule (Zonation CAZ): score a zone by the single species that depends on it most, maxs ws·shares,z. Richness-free and endemism-driven; it protects every species' core but ignores aggregation of many species.
  • Damped richness: total ÷ nα with α between 0 (v10) and 1 (mean share). Whether any α is "optimal" is taken up below: none is, in the literature or on the v9 numbers; the value is a stated choice, and the profile across α is what to report.
  • Keep the total, publish the factors. The recommendation here: the total share is the quantity Section 18 asks for and the one the brief's validation targets were written against; nz,g and the mean dependence belong beside it in every table so richness is visible rather than baked in. Across categories, equal component weights already stop 9,400 invertebrates from outweighing six turtles.

Is there an optimal α?

Not in the literature. α is an order parameter, the same kind of dial as the order q of Hill numbers, which runs from richness (q = 0) through Shannon to Simpson diversity as it weights abundant species more, or Zonation's choice between summing species' proportions and taking the best remaining one. In every such family the value is a stated judgement about what should count, and the standing advice is to publish the profile across the parameter rather than pick a point on it (Hill 1973; Jost 2006; Chao et al. 2014; Moilanen 2007). At the two ends the literature does take sides: sums of range fractions (α = 0) are the quantity that selects complementary, representative sets of sites efficiently, and means lose that property (Williams et al. 1996; Albuquerque & Beier 2015); per-species means (α = 1) are used to map endemism independently of richness, at the known cost of exalting species-poor cells with a single narrow endemic (Crisp et al. 2001; Laffan & Crisp 2003).

One statistical anchor exists, and on v9 it lies outside the range. The richness-corrected-endemism idea (Crisp et al. 2001; Jetz et al. 2004) asks for the exponent β at which total ÷ nβ no longer trends with species count across the reporting units: β is the slope of log(zone total) on log(species present). On v9 that slope is – for every category, above 1: the species-rich Program Areas also hold larger mean fractions of their species, so richness and dependence reinforce each other rather than trading off, and no α between 0 and 1 removes the richness gradient. α = 1 is simply the least richness-driven member of the family; going beyond it would penalize richness outright, which the sandbox shows rewards a lone endemic. The table of β values is a useful diagnostic of how richness-coupled each category is, not a setting.

Recommendation. Publish v10 at α = 0, the quantity the brief proposes and the one with the representation argument behind it; report β per category beside the richness × dependence decomposition; and print the α profile in the report so a reader can see which conclusions survive the choice (GAA's turtle lead does at every α; the ordering of the Aleutian and Arctic areas does not). If a single compromise value is ever wanted, α = ½ is the conventional midpoint, a convention rather than an optimum.

6 · The sliver rule

When a component is present in name only

St. George Basin (GEO) is the example that started this review: under v9 it appears on the turtle map above the Aleutian Arc. Under v10 it holds – of the nation's turtle importance and – of the world's, from – species whose cells cover – of the area's surface, a strip along its southern boundary. The v9 score is already small (–), but any non-null value paints the area as turtle habitat on a map and puts a number in a table, which is the "obviously wrong" appearance a reader sees.

The rule is geometric. A component is reported for a zone only if its scored cells cover at least κ of the zone's area, where coverage is the area-weighted fraction of the zone's cells that carry a value for the category (any scored species present, at any value). Rounded up from GEO's 1.5 %, κ = 5 %: a component that occupies less than one twentieth of an area is a boundary artefact, not that area's component. Below the floor the component is not reportable: it is drawn as no data, footnoted, and left out of the composite exactly as a category with no cells already is, never counted as a zero (a zero would pull the composite down for a reason unrelated to sensitivity). The rule is defined on the footprint, not on either score, so it applies identically to v9 and v10 tables.

Why it is safe to apply universally. On v9 the next-lowest coverage after GEO's turtles is 18 %, primary producers in Northern California, a nearshore category whose cells hug the coast of every Pacific area (18–49 %); the lowest of any other category is High Arctic corals at 40 %. Any κ between 2 % and 18 % therefore removes exactly one pair, GEO's turtles, and 5 % is proposed as a round number with a wide margin below the nearshore categories that will also catch the same artefact wherever a range boundary clips a corner of a future zone set. A share floor τ (the zone holds less than τ of the nation's risk-weighted importance for the category) is a different question, importance rather than presence, in the spirit of Ramsar's and the KBA standard's population-share thresholds; it is offered here as an optional complement, off by default, because at any useful setting it also removes low-share Arctic corals and primary producers that do occupy their areas. 7 · Sandbox

A coastline of 24 cells, two ecoregions, four zones

What a sandbox is here. A cordoned-off toy world: a handful of invented species with adjustable distributions, run through five scoring rules side by side: v9, v10, and three variants kept for demonstration (a v9 national min–max, a v10 with the study-area denominator, and the mean share per species). It exists to show the statistical behaviour of each rule, how a score responds when a species is endemic, wide-ranging, peripheral, rare or numerous, without the noise of 17,000 real species. Nothing in it is data; every number is recomputed from the sliders.

The world. One coastline of 24 equal-area cells running south to north. Cells 1–12 are the southern ecoregion, 13–24 the northern one; each ecoregion holds two zones of six cells (S1, S2, N1, N2), the analogue of Program Areas. Each species is a bump along the coast with a centre, a width, a peak suitability (0–100), a risk weight (the er score, 1–100) and a share of its range outside the country (which only the global-denominator rule sees). Copies clones a species to simulate species-rich waters.

What the scenarios show

  • The v9 rule is a within-region ranking. Every ecoregion contains a 100; a faint tail of one species is enough to set it. Cross-region comparison of these numbers compares curves.
  • The national min–max variant is comparable but richness-driven. The species-poor ecoregion collapses toward zero whenever the other holds more species, whatever their status.
  • v10 counts each species once, worldwide. A species contributes at most its weight, split across its range, so a wide-ranging species can no longer inflate every zone it touches. Richness still adds: forty copies of a common species outscore one endemic unless their fractions are small (the richness-gradient scenario). The denominator decides how vagrants and peripheral species count.
  • 100 is the national average concentration. Here every zone has six cells, so a zone at 100 holds a quarter of the category; the real Program Areas differ forty-fold in area, which is why v10 divides by area (section 3).
  • Mean share cancels counts, then over-rewards species-poor zones: one endemic alone in a zone scores as high as ten endemics together.

Where the global denominator comes from, and what happens when there is none

A share needs a total to divide by. In v9 every scored species has one, from two sources: the merged global surface (range-constrained taxa: expert range ∩ suitability, whole range) or, for the AquaMaps-only taxa, AquaMaps outside US waters plus the merged surface inside (AquaX supersedes AquaMaps inside its mask). The two are not equivalent: AquaMaps over-predicts, so it inflates the denominator and deflates the US share of those species. The cell-count and suitability-weighted fractions in the turtle table show how much the weighting itself moves the number (Kemp's ridley: 46 % by suitability, 13 % by footprint).

Species with only a national or regional model (– in v9: AquaX-only fishes and invertebrates, FWS-range-only birds, fishes, shrimps and sea-otter subspecies; and every future SEFSC/NCCOS density surface) have no global total. Treated naively they become national endemics with share 1, which inflates their contribution exactly where the brief wants restraint. This page applies the R2 fallback chain to them: an endemic fraction of 1 where the taxon is a US-endemic listed entity (the Hawaiian stilt, Gulf sturgeon, tidewater goby, saltmarsh topminnow, the four Hawaiian anchialine shrimps, the southern sea otter), a literature fraction where a population estimate exists (northern sea otter, laughing gull, Eskimo curlew), and an OBIS occurrence fraction (records inside the US EEZ ÷ records worldwide) for the AquaX-only species with no range assessment at all. Each is flagged in the table below and would live in a curated data/us_share_fallback.csv in the pipeline.

9 · Reading the brief

Analytical findings, then recommendations

The brief's core is sound and well precedented: per-species normalization is the standard move in spatial conservation planning, the resulting index is rarity-weighted richness with a suitability surface and a risk weight, and a single national rescale is the only way to make one column mean the same thing in every row. The findings are what the v9 numbers say; the recommendations are what I would decide, with the literature that backs each.

9a · Analytical findings

  1. Step 1 (strip the floor) will not do most of the work. The brief expects the floor of 1 across the SWOT range to be what paints Alaska. In v9 the leatherback's mean merged value in the Aleutian Arc is – on the 0–100 scale, not 1, and the East Bering Sea's raw turtle ceiling (max cell –) is a third of the Eastern Gulf's (–). The suitability model multiplied by an Endangered weight is the source; Formula 9.4 then makes both ceilings 100. Stripping the floor removes noise; the denominator removes the illusion.
  2. Step 1's "TSS cutoff" would binarize the suitability surface, which is a different operation from the one the brief describes. The True Skill Statistic is a skill measure of a presence/absence prediction, sensitivity + specificity − 1, ranging from −1 to +1 and independent of prevalence (Allouche et al. 2006). What AquaX delivers per species beside its TSS is a cutoff on the suitability scale, the threshold at which TSS is maximized (the max-SSS rule, Liu et al. 2013), recorded in v9's model_ax.csv and applied only when AX_APPLY_CUTOFF=1. "Treating sub-threshold cells as absences" therefore turns a continuous suitability into a presence/absence map at a species-specific, sample-dependent threshold that drops about half of a typical model's pixels. Inside a share it changes numerator and denominator alike, throws away the information the share is built to use, and moves each species toward its cell-count fraction (turtle table: Kemp's ridley 46 % by suitability, 13 % by footprint). Thresholding does not improve a suitability estimate and is not needed for prioritization on continuous outputs (Guillera-Arroita et al. 2015). The right use of the cutoff is as an optional range footprint, applied consistently to numerator and denominator and reported as a sensitivity, not as a step in the metric.
  3. Step 4 is not a percent yet. The fractions of a category sum to Σs ws/100, not to 1, and Program Areas do not tile the study area. The added pct line in section 2 fixes it; Step 5 is unaffected.
  4. A share is a total, so area counts. Steps 2–5 sum fractions over a zone's cells, which makes the score extensive; the published score is an area-weighted mean, an intensity. On v9 the Aleutian Arc (– km²) moves from composite rank – to – under the total share, Cook Inlet (– km²) from – to –, Hope Basin from – to –. The per-km² share correlates with the published ranking at ρ = – against – for the total.
  5. The share rewards endemism and is blind to hotspots of wide-ranging species. Humpback critical habitat contributes little because the humpback's range is enormous (– of it in US waters); a Cook Inlet beluga or Rice's whale dominates its area. In planning terms the share is an irreplaceability measure (Kukkala & Moilanen 2013), the ecoregional score a within-region density measure; richness, endemism and threat hotspots do not coincide (Orme et al. 2005). They are different maps.
  6. Equal category weights amplify small components. Six turtles and one Gulf endemic (Kemp's ridley, – of its global range in US waters) move the composite as much as 9,400 invertebrates.
  7. The denominator is itself a modelling choice. Two sources feed it in v9 (merged global surface vs AquaMaps outside US waters), – species have no global model at all and take the R2 fallback (endemic, literature or OBIS-occurrence fraction; section 8), and the suitability-weighted and footprint fractions differ several-fold for the same species (turtle table).
  8. v9 variants do not resolve the comparability problem. Percentile endpoints in Formula 9.4 change several published scores (Gulf of Alaska turtles – → –) while leaving every ecoregion with its own 100 (composite ρ with v9 = –); a single national min–max is comparable but richness-driven (section 5). Both are kept on the page as demonstrations only.
  9. The brief's validation targets pass on v9 with the global denominator. Alaska Program Areas together hold – of the nation's turtle importance, the two Gulf areas –; GAA is the national 100; Rice's whale is the top mammal contributor in –; the Arctic keeps its mammal columns through bowhead, walrus and the ice seals. With the US denominator Alaska holds – and the Gulf of Alaska alone exceeds the brief's 1 % target, because the leatherback's US range is mostly Alaskan water.

9b · Recommendations and decisions

  1. v10 is the cross-region score; the v9 ecoregional score stays for within-region use. Why keep it: inside one ecoregion Formula 9.4 is a linear transform of the raw cell score, so the ranking it gives among cells and among Program Areas of that region is the raw density ranking, and that is the right question for siting inside a region, for the cell-level maps, and for continuity with every published report. v10 ranks by irreplaceability instead and under-serves the dense habitat of wide-ranging species (Moilanen 2007: additive benefit functions favour feature-rich cells, core-area rules favour rarity; Veach et al. 2017; Orme et al. 2005). Two labelled numbers, "relative within ecoregion" and "share of range", each answering its own question; never one national column beside six ecoregional ones.
    Moilanen 2007; Kukkala & Moilanen 2013; Orme et al. 2005; Veach et al. 2017; OECD/JRC 2008 on mixing scales.
  2. Denominator: the global range, with the study-area share as a second column. With a study-area denominator a species whose only US cells are a sliver has share 1.0 in that sliver: the vagrant problem returns at species level (leatherback: 2 % of its range in US waters, mostly Alaska; Aleutian Arc 2.6 % of the US turtle share but 0.2 % of the global). The global denominator is what the brief's own concept paragraph promises and how every population-share threshold in conservation policy is written: Ramsar's 1 % of a waterbird population, the KBA standard's 0.5–10 % of the global population, marine IBAs and IMMAs. It imports a "national responsibility" weighting, which the responsibility literature recommends and asks to be stated with its reference area. For species without a global model, use, in order: the merged global surface; the AquaMaps global footprint; the expert-range footprint (IUCN, BirdLife, SWOT cell counts, already ingested whole-range); a literature fraction of the global population or range in US waters (IUCN Red List range and EOO, BirdLife and Partners in Flight population estimates, NOAA stock assessments, NatureServe "national responsibility" ranks); and only then the endemic assumption, flagged in metric.description and shown as a struck-through pill in the species app, as an input without a surface is today.
    Schmeller et al. 2008, 2014; Laffan & Crisp 2003; Ramsar Criterion 6; IUCN 2016; Donald et al. 2019; Lesica & Allendorf 1995 for the caveat.
  3. Publish v10 at α = 0 and report the α profile, not an "optimal" α. No ecological optimum exists for the richness damping exponent: it is an order parameter like Hill's q, and the literature's advice is to show the profile. The one statistical anchor, the exponent that decouples the score from species count, lies above 1 for every v9 category, so no admissible α removes richness; α = 0 keeps the representation argument, and β per category is reported as a diagnostic (section 5).
    Hill 1973; Jost 2006; Chao et al. 2014; Moilanen 2007; Williams et al. 1996; Albuquerque & Beier 2015; Crisp et al. 2001; Jetz et al. 2004.
  4. Keep the suitability surface continuous; treat the AquaX cutoff as a footprint option, not a metric step. Compute the share on the merged values as they are (the floor stripped for premultiplied taxa), and, if a threshold is wanted at all, apply it to the range footprint for numerator and denominator together and publish the difference as a sensitivity column. Report AUC and TSS as model quality in the species app, where they belong.
    Allouche et al. 2006; Liu et al. 2013; Guillera-Arroita et al. 2015.
  5. Reference point: 100 = the national average concentration, the same for every area and component (section 3). A fixed reference that exists in the data itself; no best-performer stretch, so no area's score depends on another's, no component has a built-in 100, and scores stay comparable across releases. The total share of the world's importance is printed beside every score as the amount at stake, and a policy bar (the 1 % of a global population that Ramsar and the KBA standard use) is its natural reference.
    Halpern et al. 2012 and Samhouri et al. 2012 on reference points; OECD/JRC 2008 on distance-to-reference normalization; Morandi et al. 2018 (OFWESA's fixed maxima); Isserman 1977 (location quotient); IUCN 2016 and Ramsar for the 1 % bar.
  6. Component weights: equal, published with a sensitivity analysis (section 4). Species-count weighting double-counts richness and hands most of the composite to invertebrates and fishes; total-risk-weight weighting turns the composite into a listed-species exposure index. Report the composite and ranks under both, and under a regulatory and a biodiversity stakeholder set, with the Spearman against equal weights, as OHI reported extractive versus preservationist perspectives.
    OECD/JRC 2008; Halpern et al. 2012; Burgass et al. 2017; McRae et al. 2017; Lehtomäki & Moilanen 2013.
  7. Adopt the coverage floor (section 6). A component is reported for a zone only if its scored cells cover at least κ = 5 % of the zone's area; below that it is not reportable: no data on the map, a footnote in the table, excluded from the composite like a category with no cells, never a zero. Defined on the footprint, so it applies to v9 and v10 tables alike; on v9 it removes exactly one pair, St. George Basin's turtles, without a rule about Alaska, and any κ from 2 % to 18 % does the same (the nearshore primary-producer component sets the ceiling). A share floor (importance rather than presence) stays optional.
    OECD/JRC 2008 on the treatment of missing components; Ramsar Criterion 6 and IUCN 2016 for the share-threshold analogy.
  8. Quantity: the score is the intensity (per km²); the total is the companion. Sensitivity is a property of a place and must not grow with the size of the Program Area drawn around it, so v10 divides the zone total by the area's share of the nation's surface, as v9's area-weighted mean did (Formula 9.5). The total share of the world's importance, which does grow with area, is what OCSLA's "different areas" and site-designation thresholds are about, and it is printed beside every score as the amount at stake.
    43 U.S.C. § 1344(a)(2)(G); OECD/JRC 2008 on scale effects; Stock & Micheli 2016 on sum-vs-mean aggregation; Bradbury et al. 2014 (sensitivity as a density).
  9. Print the Spearman correlation between the v9 and v10 rankings with the re-issued table. The other standing v1 recommendation, percentile endpoints in Formula 9.4, is moot under v10, which uses no min–max; it is shown on this page only as a v9 variant.
    OECD/JRC 2008 (robustness reporting); the v1 report's own recommendation.
  10. Composite weights: keep equal weights, state them as policy, and report the sensitivity to a Σ-weight or species-count weighting, a one-line variant. Equal goal weights are the Ocean Health Index convention the flower plot borrows; what makes OHI comparable across regions is that its reference points are, which is what the share restores.
    Halpern et al. 2012; OECD/JRC 2008 on compensability.
  11. RMU population weighting (brief step 6) only after the denominator is settled. With a global denominator the North Pacific loggerhead and West Pacific leatherback are already small fractions in Alaska; a second down-weight on the same species needs an abundance argument, not a geographic one. Observations (Hodge & Wing 2000; OBIS-SEAMAP; observer records) validate the post-fix Alaska share; they are not a surface.
    Wallace et al. 2023; Hodge & Wing 2000.

10 · Literature

What the method is called elsewhere, and what that literature warns about

Synthesis

Three bodies of work converge on the brief's arithmetic. Biogeography has computed "the fraction of each species' range in a cell, summed over species" since the 1990s under the names range-size rarity, weighted endemism and rarity-weighted richness (Williams et al. 1996; Crisp et al. 2001). It established the two properties that matter here: the sum is a reliable surrogate for complementarity-based site selection, on average slightly more efficient than Zonation itself (Albuquerque & Beier 2015), and it is not the same map as richness or threat, so priorities set on richness lose coverage that rarity-weighting keeps (Orme et al. 2005; Veach et al. 2017; Astudillo-Scalia & Albuquerque 2020). Systematic conservation planning then made per-feature normalization the entry step of every prioritization: Zonation expresses each layer as a proportion of that feature's total before anything else happens, so species counts cannot dominate and species weights, the MST's extinction-risk scores, multiply proportions rather than raw values (Moilanen et al. 2005). Its benefit-function work is also the clearest statement of the brief's remaining choice: summing proportions (the additive benefit function, the brief's Step 3) favours feature-rich cells, while taking the best remaining proportion per species (core-area Zonation) protects each species' core at the expense of richness (Moilanen 2007; Lehtomäki & Moilanen 2013). Conservation policy already writes its site thresholds as fractions of a global or biogeographic population, 1 % for Ramsar and marine IBAs, 0.5–10 % for Key Biodiversity Areas, which is why the global denominator is the natural reading of "share" and why national-responsibility assessments insist the reference area be stated (Schmeller et al. 2008, 2014).

The offshore-energy sensitivity indices the MST descends from sit on the same foundation: Garthe & Hüppop's index and its Scottish and English successors weight each species by conservation status and the proportion of its biogeographic population in the area, then map sensitivity on one scale across the whole planning sea (Garthe & Hüppop 2004; Furness et al. 2013; Bradbury et al. 2014), and BOEM's own Pacific bird index carries the share of the population in the study area inside its population-vulnerability term (Adams et al. 2016; Kelsey et al. 2018). BOEM's OFWESA model is the closest precedent for the decision at hand: it abandoned the earlier RESA practice of comparing study areas to each other, normalized instead to fixed hypothetical endpoints "so that the results would be independent of the sensitivity of other regions", and averaged species-group sensitivity per species evaluated (Morandi et al. 2018). The composite-indicator literature supplies the remaining discipline: min–max normalization is outlier-sensitive, hence percentile endpoints; additive equal weights are fully compensatory and should be reported with a sensitivity analysis; and the choice between summing and averaging, or between regional and global rescaling, is one of the largest sources of uncertainty in cumulative-impact maps, so it is a choice to state, not to hide (OECD/JRC 2008; Halpern et al. 2012; Halpern et al. 2009; Stock & Micheli 2016).

Applied to the MST, v10 is therefore neither novel nor untested: it is rarity-weighted richness on a suitability surface with an extinction-risk weight and a global denominator, reported both as a total per area (the Section 18 quantity) and per km² (the intensity readers know), rescaled once across the reporting units, with the ecoregional score retained for the within-region question it was built for. The two cautions the literature attaches are the ones the findings above turn up: the score is an irreplaceability map, blind to the dense habitat of wide-ranging species, and its denominator is a modelling decision whose reference area and sources must be stated. A third, from the species-distribution-modelling literature, concerns the brief's Step 1: a "TSS cutoff" is a max-skill threshold for turning suitability into presence/absence (Allouche et al. 2006; Liu et al. 2013), and prioritization is one of the uses for which the continuous surface should be kept (Guillera-Arroita et al. 2015).

12 · In the apps

Showing v9 and v10 without showing six numbers

The risk is not too little information but too much: two scores, two denominators, two quantities and a composite would put eight numbers on every Program Area. The apps should show one question at a time and make the question the control.

  • A single "Compare" switch, not a metric picker. Within ecoregion (v9: the published score, the current map, the current flower plot) or Across the nation (v10: the concentration relative to the national average, with the amount at stake beside it). The switch relabels the legend, the flower plot's axis and every tooltip; nothing else changes on screen. The denominator and per-km² choices are settled by the method, not exposed as toggles; they live in the methods page and the manifest.
  • Say what the number means, in words, next to it. In national mode the tooltip reads "Aleutian Arc: a fifth of the national average concentration of sea turtles (rank 8 of 11 areas with turtles); 0.2 % of the world's turtle importance at stake"; in ecoregional mode "50 of 100 relative to the East Bering Sea". A number with its sentence cannot be mis-compared.
  • Cell maps switch with the mode. The national-mode raster is sharec,g per cell relative to the national mean cell (a COG like the others, one national legend, 100 = average cell); the ecoregional raster stays as it is. The manifest already carries rescale ranges per raster, so this is a second metric, not a new mechanism.
  • The treemap is where the share is explained. Per-species share is exactly what the treemap draws when the leaf value is ws × shares,z: readers see that the Gulf's turtle score is Kemp's ridley and the Arctic's mammal score is bowhead, walrus and ice seals. Keep it as the drill-down, in both modes.
  • Rank change as a badge, not a column. When a user has both modes in view (the comparison report, not the map), show the rank under each mode and a ▲▼ badge for the difference, as the dumbbell above does.
  • One composite per mode. Never average a national column with ecoregional ones; the composite in national mode is the equal-weight mean of the reportable v10 components, on the same 100 = national-average scale.
  • Flag the assumptions once. A species without a global model carries a small "national-only model" pill in the species app, the same convention as an input without a published surface; the methods page lists them.
