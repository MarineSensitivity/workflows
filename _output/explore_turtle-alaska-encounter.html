1 Bottom line

Short answer. Downweight the Bering, Chukchi, Beaufort and Arctic waters hard: no turtle has ever been recorded there, and there has been plenty of looking. Leave the Gulf of Alaska near its current value for now. The two fair comparisons for it disagree, and the Alaska Region’s records since 2002 would settle which one holds today.

  1. The three Program Area regions separate cleanly. Per unit of looking, turtles turn up in the Gulf of America 16 to 50 times as often as off California, Oregon and Washington: 18× on OBIS surveys, 16× on all OBIS + GBIF records, 50× on iNaturalist. Per marine mammal stranding, the Gulf strands about 3,600 times as many turtles as Washington. So these measures do tell a turtle coast from a cold one.
  2. Bering to Arctic: no turtle, ever, in any channel. None appears in the NOAA Alaska file (58 years), OBIS, GBIF or iNaturalist. There are 13,317 cells there with a marine mammal record and not one turtle; off CA/OR/WA, 211 of 8,988 such cells hold a turtle. The Bering-to-Arctic rate is at most 1/160 of the Pacific’s (95th percentile), and 1/1,400 at the central estimate.
  3. Gulf of Alaska: the two fair comparisons disagree.
    • Strandings, 1980–2002 (line D). 17 turtles stranded on Gulf of Alaska beaches and 32 on Washington’s. Per marine mammal stranding, Alaska’s rate is 1.6 times Washington’s (95% interval 0.9–2.9). Most were cold-stunned or dead green turtles in El Niño winters, which reached both coasts. On this evidence there is no case for a downweight.
    • iNaturalist, about 2008 on (line C). 0 turtles in 8,125 Gulf of Alaska observations of marine mammals and turtles, against 495 in 76,849 off CA/OR/WA. Alaska’s rate is at most 1/27 of the Pacific’s (95th percentile), and 1/230 at the central estimate.
    • The difference is the years covered and the reporting channel. The NOAA Alaska file goes thin after 2002. Without the Alaska Region’s records from 2003 on (ask 1), the recent silence cannot be put down to fewer turtles rather than to nobody compiling.
  4. Divide out what the SDMs already do. The model’s own Alaska-to-Pacific ratio differs a lot by species:
    • Loggerhead: the model rates the Gulf of Alaska at 1.1 times the Pacific and the East Bering Sea at 0.86, yet Alaska has two loggerhead records in 58 years. Most of the correction needed is here.
    • Leatherback: the model already rates the Gulf of Alaska at 0.055 of the Pacific and the Bering at 0.009, at or below what the records support. The weight leaves it alone.
    • Kemp’s ridley and hawksbill: these are model artefacts. Kemp’s ridley has never been recorded in the Pacific, yet the model scores Alaska at 4 to 8 times the Pacific, whose own score is near zero. It also scores the hawksbill higher in the High Arctic than off California. A range mask is the right tool for both, not a weight.
  5. What the proposed weights do. The proposal:
    • Bering to Arctic: weights from the all-records line (B+).
    • Gulf of Alaska: weights from the more generous of the strandings (D) and iNaturalist (C) lines.
    • Both: the model ratio divided out, and the 95th percentile used.
    Alaska’s share of each species’ US suitability then falls: loggerhead 39% → 19%, green 20% → 9%, hawksbill 42% → 6%, Kemp’s ridley 32% → 7%, olive ridley 20% → 8%. The leatherback stays at 1.7%. The Bering and Arctic Program Areas keep 4–17% of their turtle suitability; the Gulf of Alaska, Kodiak, Cook Inlet and Shumagin Program Areas keep about 97%. If the Gulf of Alaska took line C instead, they would keep about 30%, and Alaska’s loggerhead share would be 3.4%.

The decision this needs: whether the Gulf of Alaska’s weight comes from strandings (D, no downweight) or iNaturalist (C, a strong one), until the Alaska Region’s 2003-to-present records arrive.

2 The question

The turtle SDMs put suitable habitat across much of Alaska. Every range rule tried so far (v7.1’s AquaMaps P ≥ 0.5, the OBIS P10, hulls, buffers, the regional effort test in obis_range_constraint.qmd) is a mask: a cell keeps its turtle or loses it. Tim White asked instead (2026-10-06) for “comparable data based on effort with strandings and/or survey for the other coasts … so that we can proportionally downweight Alaska”. That needs two numbers per species and Alaska ecoregion:

  1. How often turtles are found in Alaska per unit of looking, relative to a reference coast. This is the observed ratio. The reference is the Pacific Program Areas off California, Oregon and Washington, which see the same North Pacific turtle populations. The Gulf of America is carried alongside as a coast where turtles are common, to show what “plenty of turtles” looks like on each line.
  2. How suitable the SDM already says Alaska is, relative to the same reference coast. This is the model ratio. If the model already scores the Gulf of Alaska at a twentieth of Washington/Oregon, it has done some of the downweighting itself.

The weight is the observed ratio divided by the model ratio, capped at 1:

\[ w_{s,r} = \min\left(1,\; \frac{\lambda_{s,r} / \lambda_{s,\text{ref}}}{\bar S_{s,r} / \bar S_{s,\text{ref}}}\right) \]

  • \(\lambda\) is an encounter rate: units where a turtle was recorded over units where someone who would have reported one was looking. Each \(\lambda\) gets a Jeffreys posterior, so zero records against little looking gives a wide interval, not a hard zero.
  • \(\bar S\) is the mean suitability per US-waters cell of the published OBIS ensemble.
  • \(w\) multiplies suitability in that ecoregion. Nothing is masked; the lower 48 keep \(w = 1\).
  • Central uses the posterior median of the observed ratio. Precautionary uses its 95th percentile, which is generous to Alaska.

The first version of this notebook used the observed ratio alone as the weight. That double-counts wherever the model already scores Alaska low, which for the leatherback is most of it (see What the SDMs already say).

3 The Alaska record: NOAA sightings and strandings, 1963–2020

Jenna Malek (NOAA Alaska Regional Office, stranding co-coordinator) sent these 49 rows to Tim White on 2026-10-05: the Alaska Region’s file for the MMA project. It continues the Hodge and Wing (2000) compilation, Occurrences of marine turtles in Alaska waters: 1960–1998. NOAA’s feature story Sea turtles in Alaska? Yes, we have them far north counts 48 sightings over the same years (19 green, 19 leatherback, 2 loggerhead, 4 olive ridley). Records from 2020 to the present were requested but have not arrived.

Each record is typed by how the turtle was found, so that strandings can be compared with other coasts’ stranding networks (line D):

  • stranding: a carcass, or a live turtle, on the beach or in the shallows.
  • bycatch: netted at sea, killed or released. Kept out of line D, as the West Coast and Gulf stranding counts below do not include at-sea bycatch.
  • sighting: seen at sea.

Fixes and flags applied here:

  • Fripo Island (2020): the longitude is entered as +131.23, so it is negated.
  • Dundas Island, B.C. (1981): Canadian waters.
  • Cannon Beach (1986): the coordinates (50.5, −139.75) are far offshore and fall outside US waters.
  • Noyes Island vicinity (1983): the coordinates (59.0, −134.0) are near Haines, but Noyes Island is at about 55.5° N. The record is kept, since either place is in the Gulf of Alaska ecoregion.
  • 2013 and 2020: two records have no coordinates.

The ecoregion polygons stop at the outer coast and leave out the Inside Passage, where most of these records are. So each record goes to the nearest ecoregion within 150 km, and anything south of Dixon Entrance (54.6° N) counts as Canadian.

4 Line D: strandings

Strandings are the one channel that every coast has run the same way for decades: a network that walks beaches and answers calls, logging marine mammals and sea turtles alike. Marine mammal strandings stand in for the looking: more people on more beaches report more of both. The rate is turtle strandings per 1,000 marine mammal strandings on the same coast.

The counts, all public, were gathered on 2026-10-12:

Alaska, Gulf of Alaska NOAA Alaska file above, typed stranding Alaska Region 2018 overview: 188 a year, 2006–2017, all of Alaska

Three choices keep the comparison like-for-like:

  • Alaska against Washington, over the same years, 1980–2002. Both turtle files are full compilations of every report in those years. The Washington appendices end in 2010 (green and loggerhead) and 2013 (leatherback), and the Alaska file goes thin after 2002.
  • Encounter types. The STSSN counts include cold-stunned turtles, as the Alaska and Washington ones do, but drop post-hatchlings and nesting females, which only a nesting coast produces.
  • Marine mammal years. The marine mammal counts are long-run averages from later years than the turtle windows. Both biases run toward a higher Alaska weight: Alaska’s 188 a year includes the Bering and Arctic coasts, where no turtle has been recorded, and Washington’s 578 a year (2007–2016) is likely higher than its 1980–2002 rate, while Alaska’s network was smaller then too.

5 Line A: surveys (OBIS)

The unit is a 0.5° block in one year. A unit counts as searched if it holds any non-telemetry record from a dataset that has recorded a sea turtle somewhere in the world, meaning the observers would have written a turtle down. This drops seabird-only programmes, which never record turtles. The USGS North Pacific Pelagic Seabird Database is one: it has no turtle code and no turtle in its comments, so its 50 years of Alaska transects cannot count as turtle effort. A unit counts as occupied if it holds a turtle record.

Telemetry is left out, because tags go where the tagged animals go.   g <- st_geometry(filter(ply_eco, eco == e)) |> st_union() |> st_buffer(1) |>
    st_intersection(st_as_sfc(st_bbox(BOX_C[[e]], crs = 4326))) |>
    st_simplify(dTolerance = 0.05) |> st_make_valid() |> st_cast("POLYGON")

    list(m)

Reading across the rows:

  • Gulf of America sits 16–50 times above the Pacific on the lines with direct looking, and about 3,600 times above Washington on strandings. The Gulf’s turtles include nesting beaches: STSSN’s nesting and post-hatchling records are taken out here, but iNaturalist’s nest and hatchling photographs are not, which inflates line C for the Gulf.
  • Bering to Arctic is zero on every line. Only lines B and B+ carry enough looking (13,317 cells) for that zero to mean something. Line A’s 62 block-years cannot tell, and lines C and D do not reach it.
  • Gulf of Alaska depends on the line:
    • Line A (surveys) cannot tell: 0 turtles in 58 block-years.
    • Lines B and C (OBIS + GBIF, iNaturalist) put it two orders of magnitude below the Pacific.
    • Line B+ (B plus the NOAA file) brings it to about half the Pacific.
    • Line D (strandings) puts it level with or above Washington.
    Lines B and C count what reached the public databases. The NOAA Alaska file and the WDFW Washington appendices count every report a coordinator could find.   • Loggerhead. Off Alaska, the model is close to its Pacific score: 1.1 in the Gulf of Alaska and 0.86 in the East Bering Sea. Two loggerheads have been recorded in Alaska in 58 years, against five stranded on Washington’s beaches in 1980–2002 alone.
  • Green and olive ridley. The model already takes off about two thirds: 0.27–0.38 in the Gulf of Alaska and East Bering Sea.
  • Leatherback. The model already scores Alaska at a few percent of the Pacific (0.055 in the Gulf of Alaska, 0.009 in the Bering Sea), lower than the strandings and sightings suggest. Dividing by this ratio caps its weight at 1.
  • Kemp’s ridley. The species is confined to the Gulf of America and the Atlantic, and its Pacific score is near zero (mean 1). So any Alaska score at all yields a large model ratio (4.7–7.5).
  • Hawksbill. The model scores the High Arctic (26) above the Pacific (13).

Kemp’s ridley and the hawksbill are both range errors. An encounter weight cannot fix them well, because there is no Pacific rate to compare with. A range mask can.

11 A proposed rule

Each Alaska ecoregion takes its observed ratio only from lines that compare like with like and have real looking there:

  • East Bering Sea, Chukchi and Beaufort, High Arctic: line B+. The NOAA file adds nothing here, so B+ equals B. The rate is pooled across the three, since the High Arctic alone has only 66 searched cells, and each ecoregion keeps its own model ratio.
  • Gulf of Alaska: lines C and D, taking the more generous. Line D (strandings, against Washington) wins for every species. That leaves every weight at 1 except Kemp’s ridley’s, whose model ratio is the artefact described above.
  • Left out: line A everywhere, because Alaska has too little survey effort for it, and line B in the Gulf of Alaska, because the NOAA records are missing from it.

Precautionary (the 95th percentile) is the proposed default, so that a sensitivity score does not understate where a listed species may occur.

13 Caveats

  • Season is ignored. Alaska greens are reported mostly October–December and leatherbacks July–August (NOAA). An annual weight suits an annual score; a seasonal product would need the same tables by month.
  • Time. Lines B and C carry no dates (B) or cover only about 2008 on (C); line A pools the 1950s to the 2020s; line D compares 1980–2002 in Alaska and Washington with 2016–2025 in the Gulf. Warming matters here: the first sea turtle nests ever recorded on the US West Coast were laid by olive ridleys at Seal Beach, California, in September 2026 (NOAA). Weights fitted to the past will understate a northward shift.
  • The effort definition carries the result, as it did in the regional test. Seabird-only surveys (NPPSD and much of OBIS’s Alaska avifauna) are excluded because they never record turtles. Counting them would shrink the Alaska weights a great deal without justification.
  • Generalising. The same function gives a weight for every species × ecoregion against that species’ best-recorded US region, which would make this a candidate rule for the national score. If adopted it belongs in msens (encounter_weight()) with fixtures: zero detections, the pooled fallback, the model-ratio division and the cap at 1.

## Bottom line

::: {.callout-important appearance="simple"}
**Short answer.** Downweight the Bering, Chukchi, Beaufort and Arctic waters hard: no turtle has
ever been recorded there, and there has been plenty of looking. Leave the Gulf of Alaska near its
current value for now. The two fair comparisons for it disagree, and the Alaska Region's records
since 2002 would settle which one holds today.

## The question

The turtle SDMs put suitable habitat across much of Alaska. Every range rule tried so far (v7.1's
AquaMaps P ≥ 0.5, the OBIS P10, hulls, buffers, the regional effort test in
`obis_range_constraint.qmd`) is a **mask**: a cell keeps its turtle or loses it. Tim White asked
instead (2026-10-06) for "comparable data based on effort with strandings and/or survey for the
other coasts … so that we can proportionally downweight Alaska". That needs two numbers per species
and Alaska ecoregion:

1. **How often turtles are found in Alaska per unit of looking, relative to a reference coast.**
   This is the *observed ratio*. The reference is the Pacific Program Areas off California, Oregon
   and Washington, which see the same North Pacific turtle populations. The Gulf of America is
   carried alongside as a coast where turtles are common, to show what "plenty of turtles" looks
   like on each line.
2. **How suitable the SDM already says Alaska is, relative to the same reference coast.** This is
   the *model ratio*. If the model already scores the Gulf of Alaska at a twentieth of
   Washington/Oregon, it has done some of the downweighting itself.

The weight is the observed ratio divided by the model ratio, capped at 1:

$$
w_{s,r} = \min\left(1,\; \frac{\lambda_{s,r} / \lambda_{s,\text{ref}}}{\bar S_{s,r} / \bar S_{s,\text{ref}}}\right)
$$

- $\lambda$ is an **encounter rate**: units where a turtle was recorded over units where someone who
  would have reported one was looking. Each $\lambda$ gets a Jeffreys posterior, so zero records
  against little looking gives a wide interval, not a hard zero.
- $\bar S$ is the mean suitability per US-waters cell of the published OBIS ensemble.
- $w$ multiplies suitability in that ecoregion. Nothing is masked; the lower 48 keep $w = 1$.
- *Central* uses the posterior median of the observed ratio. *Precautionary* uses its 95th
  percentile, which is generous to Alaska.

The first version of this notebook used the observed ratio alone as the weight. That double-counts
wherever the model already scores Alaska low, which for the leatherback is most of it (see
[What the SDMs already say](#what-the-sdms-already-say)).

**Lines of evidence.** A ratio is only fair when the turtles and the looking come through the same
reporting channel, so each line pairs a turtle count with an effort count from that channel:

## The Alaska record: NOAA sightings and strandings, 1963–2020

Jenna Malek (NOAA Alaska Regional Office, stranding co-coordinator) sent these 49 rows to Tim White
on 2026-10-05: the Alaska Region's file for the MMA project. It continues the Hodge and Wing (2000)
compilation, *Occurrences of marine turtles in Alaska waters: 1960–1998*. NOAA's feature story
[*Sea turtles in Alaska? Yes, we have them far north*](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/feature-story/sea-turtles-alaska-yes-we-have-them-far-north)
counts 48 sightings over the same years (19 green, 19 leatherback, 2 loggerhead, 4 olive ridley).
Records from 2020 to the present were requested but have not arrived.

Each record is typed by how the turtle was found, so that strandings can be compared with other
coasts' stranding networks (line D):

- **stranding**: a carcass, or a live turtle, on the beach or in the shallows.
- **bycatch**: netted at sea, killed or released. Kept out of line D, as the West Coast and Gulf
  stranding counts below do not include at-sea bycatch.
- **sighting**: seen at sea.

Fixes and flags applied here:

- **Fripo Island (2020):** the longitude is entered as +131.23, so it is negated.
- **Dundas Island, B.C. (1981):** Canadian waters.
- **Cannon Beach (1986):** the coordinates (50.5, −139.75) are far offshore and fall outside US waters.
- **Noyes Island vicinity (1983):** the coordinates (59.0, −134.0) are near Haines, but Noyes
  Island is at about 55.5° N. The record is kept, since either place is in the Gulf of Alaska
  ecoregion.
- **2013 and 2020:** two records have no coordinates.

The ecoregion polygons stop at the outer coast and leave out the Inside Passage, where most of these
records are. So each record goes to the nearest ecoregion within 150 km, and anything south of Dixon
Entrance (54.6° N) counts as Canadian.

## Line D: strandings

Strandings are the one channel that every coast has run the same way for decades: a network that
walks beaches and answers calls, logging marine mammals and sea turtles alike. Marine mammal
strandings stand in for the looking: more people on more beaches report more of both. The
rate is turtle strandings per 1,000 marine mammal strandings on the same coast.

The counts, all public, were gathered on 2026-10-12:

| coast | turtle strandings | marine mammal strandings |
|---|---|---|
| Gulf of America, TX to FL Gulf coast | [STSSN data application](https://connect.fisheries.noaa.gov/content/cb3f4647-9e4f-4f3d-9edf-e7a87a1feef6/), 2016–2025, by state, species and encounter type | Litz (2015), [Gulf cetacean strandings 2000–2014](https://www.mmc.gov/wp-content/uploads/Litz_overview-_cs_data_gom_0415.pdf), Monroe County FL to Texas: 375 a year |
| Washington outer coast | WDFW periodic status reviews ([green and loggerhead](https://wdfw.wa.gov/publications/01870), [leatherback](https://wdfw.wa.gov/sites/default/files/publications/01871/draft_wdfw01871.pdf)): every Washington record, 1950–2013, Appendix A | West Coast Region [10-year summary](https://media.fisheries.noaa.gov/dam-migration/mm_stranding_10yeardata_q_a_final_2018.pdf): 578 a year, 2007–2016 |
| Alaska, Gulf of Alaska | NOAA Alaska file above, typed `stranding` | Alaska Region [2018 overview](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/37538): 188 a year, 2006–2017, all of Alaska |
| California, Oregon | not public; requested from the West Coast Region | 3,357 and 378 a year, 2007–2016 |

Three choices keep the comparison like-for-like:

- **Alaska against Washington, over the same years, 1980–2002.** Both turtle files are full
  compilations of every report in those years. The Washington appendices end in 2010 (green and
  loggerhead) and 2013 (leatherback), and the Alaska file goes thin after 2002.
- **Encounter types.** The STSSN counts include cold-stunned turtles, as the Alaska and Washington
  ones do, but drop post-hatchlings and nesting females, which only a nesting coast produces.
- **Marine mammal years.** The marine mammal counts are long-run averages from later years than
  the turtle windows. Both biases run toward a *higher* Alaska weight: Alaska's 188 a year includes
  the Bering and Arctic coasts, where no turtle has been recorded, and Washington's 578 a year
  (2007–2016) is likely higher than its 1980–2002 rate, while Alaska's network was smaller then too.

## Line A: surveys (OBIS)

The unit is a 0.5° block in one year. A unit counts as **searched** if it holds any non-telemetry
record from a dataset that has recorded a sea turtle somewhere in the world, meaning the observers
would have written a turtle down. This drops seabird-only programmes, which never record turtles.
The USGS North Pacific Pelagic Seabird Database is one: it has no turtle code and no turtle in its
comments, so its 50 years of Alaska transects cannot count as turtle effort. A unit counts as
**occupied** if it holds a turtle record.

Telemetry is left out, because tags go where the tagged animals go. That means `MachineObservation`,
which also carries placeholder fixes at Seattle (47.5, −122.5), plus any dataset whose name says
telemetry, tracking or photo-ID. The TOPP state-space track summary, filed as `HumanObservation`,
was otherwise most of the "Alaska effort".

The model ratios explain why the weight has to divide them out:

- **Loggerhead.** Off Alaska, the model is close to its Pacific score: 1.1 in the Gulf of Alaska and
  0.86 in the East Bering Sea. Two loggerheads have been recorded in Alaska in 58 years, against
  five stranded on Washington's beaches in 1980–2002 alone.
- **Green and olive ridley.** The model already takes off about two thirds: 0.27–0.38 in the Gulf of
  Alaska and East Bering Sea.
- **Leatherback.** The model already scores Alaska at a few percent of the Pacific (0.055 in the Gulf
  of Alaska, 0.009 in the Bering Sea), lower than the strandings and sightings suggest. Dividing by
  this ratio caps its weight at 1.
- **Kemp's ridley.** The species is confined to the Gulf of America and the Atlantic, and its Pacific
  score is near zero (mean 1). So any Alaska score at all yields a large model ratio (4.7–7.5).
- **Hawksbill.** The model scores the High Arctic (26) above the Pacific (13).

Kemp's ridley and the hawksbill are both range errors. An encounter weight cannot fix them well,
because there is no Pacific rate to compare with. A range mask can.

lines the loggerhead and olive ridley: they are too rarely recorded on the West Coast to pin down a
reference rate of their own.

## A proposed rule

Each Alaska ecoregion takes its observed ratio only from lines that compare like with like and
have real looking there:

- **East Bering Sea, Chukchi and Beaufort, High Arctic:** line B+. The NOAA file adds nothing here,
  so B+ equals B. The rate is pooled across the three, since the High Arctic alone has only 66
  searched cells, and each ecoregion keeps its own model ratio.
- **Gulf of Alaska:** lines C and D, taking the more generous. Line D (strandings, against
  Washington) wins for every species. That leaves every weight at 1 except Kemp's ridley's, whose
  model ratio is the artefact described above.
- **Left out:** line A everywhere, because Alaska has too little survey effort for it, and line B in
  the Gulf of Alaska, because the NOAA records are missing from it.

Precautionary (the 95th percentile) is the proposed default, so that a sensitivity score does not
understate where a listed species may occur.

## Caveats

- **Season is ignored.** Alaska greens are reported mostly October–December and leatherbacks
  July–August (NOAA). An annual weight suits an annual score; a seasonal product would need the
  same tables by month.
- **Time.** Lines B and C carry no dates (B) or cover only about 2008 on (C); line A pools the 1950s
  to the 2020s; line D compares 1980–2002 in Alaska and Washington with 2016–2025 in the Gulf.
  Warming matters here: the first sea turtle nests ever recorded on the US West Coast were laid
  by olive ridleys at Seal Beach, California, in September 2026
  ([NOAA](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/feature-story/first-ever-nesting-olive-ridley-sea-turtles-california)).
  Weights fitted to the past will understate a northward shift.
- **The effort definition carries the result**, as it did in the regional test. Seabird-only
  surveys (NPPSD and much of OBIS's Alaska avifauna) are excluded because they never record turtles.
  Counting them would shrink the Alaska weights a great deal without justification.
- **Generalising.** The same function gives a weight for every species × ecoregion against that
  species' best-recorded US region, which would make this a candidate rule for the national score.
  If adopted it belongs in msens (`encounter_weight()`) with fixtures: zero detections, the pooled
  fallback, the model-ratio division and the cap at 1.
