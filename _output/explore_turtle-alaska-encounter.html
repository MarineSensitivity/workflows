1 Why

Every range rule tried so far (v7.1’s AquaMaps P ≥ 0.5, the OBIS P10, hulls, buffers, the regional effort test in obis_range_constraint.qmd) is a mask: a cell keeps its turtle or loses it. This notebook tries the other idea from the 2026-10-06 thread with Tim White, “comparable data based on effort with strandings and/or survey for the other coasts … so that we can proportionally downweight Alaska”. The suitability stays, scaled by how often turtles are actually encountered per unit of looking, compared with the US West Coast, where the same North Pacific populations are recorded.

\[ w_{s,r} = \min\left(1,\; \frac{\lambda_{s,r}}{\lambda_{s,\text{ref}}}\right), \qquad \lambda_{s,r} = \frac{\text{units in region } r \text{ where species } s \text{ was recorded}}{\text{units in region } r \text{ where someone who would have reported it looked}} \]

  • \(s\) is a species, \(r\) an ecoregion and ref the US West Coast (California Current + Washington/Oregon).
  • Each \(\lambda\) gets a Jeffreys Beta(½, ½) posterior, so a region with zero records gets an upper bound set by its effort rather than a hard zero.
  • \(w\) is reported two ways. Central is the posterior median of the ratio. Precautionary is its 95th percentile, which is generous to Alaska.
  • The weight multiplies suitability, so a cell keeps its turtle at a fraction of its value.

The ratio is only fair when the numerator and the denominator come from the same reporting channel, so the notebook builds three lines of evidence from real data. A fourth, strandings, is mocked up with the Alaska half filled in:

2 The Alaska record: NOAA sightings and strandings, 1963–2020

Jenna Malek (NOAA Alaska Regional Office, stranding co-coordinator) sent these 49 rows to Tim White on 2026-10-05: the Alaska Region’s file for the MMA project. The file is the long-running Wing/Hodge compilation. NOAA’s feature story Sea turtles in Alaska? Yes, we have them far north counts 48 sightings over the same years (19 green, 19 leatherback, 2 loggerhead, 4 olive ridley). Records from 2020 to the present were requested but have not arrived.

Fixes and flags applied here:

  • Fripo Island (2020): the longitude is entered as +131.23, so it is negated.
  • Dundas Island, B.C. (1981): Canadian waters.
  • Cannon Beach (1986): the coordinates (50.5, −139.75) are far offshore and fall outside US waters.
  • Noyes Island vicinity (1983): the coordinates (59.0, −134.0) are near Haines, but Noyes Island is at about 55.5° N. The record is kept, since either place is in the Gulf of Alaska ecoregion.
  • 2013 and 2020: two records have no coordinates.

The ecoregion polygons stop at the outer coast and leave out the Inside Passage, where most of these records are. So each record goes to the nearest ecoregion within 150 km, and anything south of Dixon Entrance (54.6° N) counts as Canadian.

3 Line A: surveys (OBIS)

The unit is a 0.5° block in one year. A unit counts as searched if it holds any non-telemetry record from a dataset that has recorded a sea turtle somewhere in the world, meaning the observers would have written a turtle down. This drops seabird-only programmes, which never record turtles. The USGS North Pacific Pelagic Seabird Database is one: it has no turtle code and no turtle in its comments, so its 50 years of Alaska transects cannot count as turtle effort. A unit counts as occupied if it holds a turtle record.

Telemetry is left out, because tags go where the tagged animals go. That means MachineObservation, which also carries placeholder fixes at Seattle (47.5, −122.5), plus any dataset whose name says telemetry, tracking or photo-ID. The TOPP state-space track summary, filed as HumanObservation, was otherwise most of the “Alaska effort”.

5 Line C: public reports (iNaturalist via GBIF)

The Alaska file is mostly public reports: fishers, beachcombers, newspapers. iNaturalist is the nearest lower-48 equivalent, so line C counts iNaturalist observations for each Pacific ecoregion. The ecoregion polygons stop at the outer coast, so each one is buffered by 1° to take in sounds, passages and beaches, then cut to its stretch of US coast. The query polygon cannot cross the antimeridian, so this line covers the Gulf of Alaska and the West Coast only.

ccw_wkt <- function(e) {   # gbif wants counter-clockwise outer rings
  g <- st_geometry(filter(ply_eco, eco == e)) |> st_union() |> st_buffer(1) |>
    st_intersection(st_as_sfc(st_bbox(BOX_C[[e]], crs = 4326))) |>
    st_simplify(dTolerance = 0.05) |> st_make_valid() |> st_cast("POLYGON")
  g <- g[order(-as.numeric(st_area(g)))][seq_len(min(3, length(g)))]   # the 3 largest parts
  rings <- map(g, function(p) {
    m <- p[[1]]; a <- sum(m[-1, 1] * m[-nrow(m), 2] - m[-nrow(m), 1] * m[-1, 2])   # shoelace (sign = orientation)
    if (a < 0) m <- m[nrow(m):1, ]
    list(m)
  })
  st_as_text(st_multipolygon(rings), digits = 7)
}

10 What this does and does not show

So far, any-turtle weights for Alaska pooled (central, precautionary):

  • Lines with real Alaska effort agree that turtles are rarer there per unit of looking. All records (B) gives 0.00057, 0.0047, and iNaturalist (C) gives 0.0043, 0.037. The Gulf of Alaska’s share of US turtle suitability falls by one to two orders of magnitude.

  • Folding in the NOAA records (B+) is the generous bound. It gives 0.098, 0.13, and the Gulf of Alaska alone sits near half the West Coast rate. The truth for the NOAA file is somewhere between B and B+, and line D is the test that would place it.

  • OBIS surveys (A) cannot tell yet. OBIS has 120 turtle-aware Alaska block-years, so the zero there is not yet evidence.

  • The rule. One multiplicative weight per species × ecoregion, from occupied over searched units, relative to the West Coast. Nothing is masked. Critical habitat and the lower 48 are untouched, and a single Alaska record cannot flip a cell on or off. The weight shrinks only as fast as the Alaska effort justifies.

  • The answer depends on the reporting channel. Surveys (A) and public reports (C) are like-for-like. All records (B) is not, unless the NOAA file is held against an equivalent lower-48 file, and B+ shows how far that moves things. Line D, strandings normalized by marine mammal strandings, is the like-for-like test for the NOAA file and needs the requested counts.

  • The effort definition carries the result, as it did in the regional test. Seabird-only surveys (NPPSD and much of OBIS’s Alaska avifauna) are excluded because they never record turtles. Counting them would shrink the Alaska weights a great deal without justification.

  • Season is ignored. Alaska greens are reported mostly October–December and leatherbacks July–August (NOAA). An annual weight is the right scale for an annual score, but a seasonal product would need the same table by month.

  • Time is ignored on lines B and C, and line A pools 1950s–2020s. Restricting to 1990 on could be checked.

  • Generalising. The same function gives a weight for every species × ecoregion against that species’ best-recorded US region, which would make this a candidate rule for the national score. If adopted it belongs in msens (encounter_weight()) with fixtures: zero detections, the pooled fallback, the cap at 1.

## Why

Every range rule tried so far (v7.1's AquaMaps P ≥ 0.5, the OBIS P10, hulls, buffers, the regional
effort test in `obis_range_constraint.qmd`) is a **mask**: a cell keeps its turtle or loses it. This
notebook tries the other idea from the 2026-10-06 thread with Tim White, "comparable data based on
effort with strandings and/or survey for the other coasts … so that we can proportionally downweight
Alaska". The suitability stays, scaled by how often turtles are actually encountered per unit of
looking, compared with the US West Coast, where the same North Pacific populations are recorded.

$$
w_{s,r} = \min\left(1,\; \frac{\lambda_{s,r}}{\lambda_{s,\text{ref}}}\right), \qquad
\lambda_{s,r} = \frac{\text{units in region } r \text{ where species } s \text{ was recorded}}{\text{units in region } r \text{ where someone who would have reported it looked}}
$$

- $s$ is a species, $r$ an ecoregion and ref the US West Coast (California Current + Washington/Oregon).
- Each $\lambda$ gets a Jeffreys Beta(½, ½) posterior, so a region with zero records gets an upper
  bound set by its effort rather than a hard zero.
- $w$ is reported two ways. *Central* is the posterior median of the ratio. *Precautionary* is its
  95th percentile, which is generous to Alaska.
- The weight multiplies suitability, so a cell keeps its turtle at a fraction of its value.

The ratio is only fair when the numerator and the denominator come from the **same reporting
channel**, so the notebook builds three lines of evidence from real data. A fourth, strandings, is
mocked up with the Alaska half filled in:

| line | records (numerator) | looking (denominator) | unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. surveys | OBIS turtle records | OBIS records from datasets that have recorded a sea turtle anywhere; telemetry and photo-ID excluded | 0.5° block × year |
| B. all records | OBIS + GBIF turtle records (`speciesgrids`) | cells with any marine mammal or sea turtle record | 0.05° cell |
| C. public reports | iNaturalist turtle observations (GBIF API) | iNaturalist marine mammal + sea turtle observations | observation |
| D. strandings | NOAA Alaska Region turtle carcasses and bycatch, 1963–2020 | marine mammal strandings, same region and years | stranding |

## The Alaska record: NOAA sightings and strandings, 1963–2020

Jenna Malek (NOAA Alaska Regional Office, stranding co-coordinator) sent these 49 rows to Tim White
on 2026-10-05: the Alaska Region's file for the MMA project. The file is the long-running
Wing/Hodge compilation. NOAA's feature story
[*Sea turtles in Alaska? Yes, we have them far north*](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/feature-story/sea-turtles-alaska-yes-we-have-them-far-north)
counts 48 sightings over the same years (19 green, 19 leatherback, 2 loggerhead, 4 olive ridley).
Records from 2020 to the present were requested but have not arrived.

Fixes and flags applied here:

- **Fripo Island (2020):** the longitude is entered as +131.23, so it is negated.
- **Dundas Island, B.C. (1981):** Canadian waters.
- **Cannon Beach (1986):** the coordinates (50.5, −139.75) are far offshore and fall outside US waters.
- **Noyes Island vicinity (1983):** the coordinates (59.0, −134.0) are near Haines, but Noyes
  Island is at about 55.5° N. The record is kept, since either place is in the Gulf of Alaska
  ecoregion.
- **2013 and 2020:** two records have no coordinates.

The ecoregion polygons stop at the outer coast and leave out the Inside Passage, where most of these
records are. So each record goes to the nearest ecoregion within 150 km, and anything south of Dixon
Entrance (54.6° N) counts as Canadian.

## Line A: surveys (OBIS)

The unit is a 0.5° block in one year. A unit counts as **searched** if it holds any non-telemetry
record from a dataset that has recorded a sea turtle somewhere in the world, meaning the observers
would have written a turtle down. This drops seabird-only programmes, which never record turtles.
The USGS North Pacific Pelagic Seabird Database is one: it has no turtle code and no turtle in its
comments, so its 50 years of Alaska transects cannot count as turtle effort. A unit counts as
**occupied** if it holds a turtle record.

Telemetry is left out, because tags go where the tagged animals go. That means `MachineObservation`,
which also carries placeholder fixes at Seattle (47.5, −122.5), plus any dataset whose name says
telemetry, tracking or photo-ID. The TOPP state-space track summary, filed as `HumanObservation`,
was otherwise most of the "Alaska effort".

## Line C: public reports (iNaturalist via GBIF)

The Alaska file is mostly public reports: fishers, beachcombers, newspapers. iNaturalist is the
nearest lower-48 equivalent, so line C counts iNaturalist observations for each Pacific ecoregion.
The ecoregion polygons stop at the outer coast, so each one is buffered by 1° to take in sounds,
passages and beaches, then cut to its stretch of US coast. The query polygon cannot cross the
antimeridian, so this line covers the Gulf of Alaska and the West Coast only.

    list(m)

## The Alaska weights

$w$ compares each Alaska ecoregion with the West Coast, species by species. A species with fewer than
`r MIN_REF` reference detections on a line uses the any-turtle rate instead. Kemp's ridley, the
hawksbill and, on most lines, the loggerhead and olive ridley are recorded too rarely off the West
Coast to pin down a reference rate. That rarity says nothing about Alaska; it only means their
reference is unknown. The any-turtle reference is dominated by green turtles, mostly the resident
San Diego Bay and Long Beach greens on line C.

Read across the lines (any-turtle weights in brackets: central, precautionary):

- **A (surveys)** is effort-matched by design, but OBIS holds almost no turtle-aware survey effort
  in Alaska. All of it is the Lamont-Doherty/LGL seismic cruises of 2004–2008 with
  protected-species observers, `r nr("A. surveys", "AK")` block-years in all against
  `r format(nr("A. surveys", "CAC") + nr("A. surveys", "WAOR"), big.mark = ",")` off the West Coast. So Alaska's zero barely
  constrains anything (pooled Alaska `r wv("A. surveys", "AK")`, `r wv("A. surveys", "AK", "w_prec")`).
  The NOAA AFSC and Marine Mammal Lab cetacean surveys, Platforms of Opportunity and fishery
  observers would make this line count, and they are the survey ask.
- **B (all records)** has `r format(nr("B. all records", "AK"), big.mark = ",")` Alaska cells with a
  marine mammal or turtle record, and not one turtle. Its weights are the smallest
  (`r wv("B. all records", "AK")`, `r wv("B. all records", "AK", "w_prec")`).
- **B+** puts the NOAA records into GBIF as though they had always been there. The Gulf of Alaska
  climbs back toward the West Coast rate (`r wv("B+. all records + NOAA AK", "GOA")`,
  `r wv("B+. all records + NOAA AK", "GOA", "w_prec")`). So line B's Alaska weight measures the
  records' absence from GBIF as much as turtles' absence from Alaska. The NOAA numerator is 58 years
  of every reporting channel, while the mammal denominator is whatever reached GBIF. Because of that
  mismatch, B+ is a generous bound rather than an estimate, and the honest comparison for the NOAA
  file is line D.
- **C (public reports)** is like-for-like on reporting: `r format(nr("C. public reports", "GOA"), big.mark = ",")`
  iNaturalist mammal and turtle observations in the Gulf of Alaska, none of them a turtle, against
  `r format(nr("C. public reports", "CAC") + nr("C. public reports", "WAOR"), big.mark = ",")` on the
  West Coast, of which `r percent(d_rate |> filter(line == "C. public reports", eco %in% ECO_REF, aphia == 0L) |> with(sum(x) / sum(n)), 0.01)`
  are turtles
  (`r wv("C. public reports", "GOA")`, `r wv("C. public reports", "GOA", "w_prec")`). It covers
  roughly the last 15 years, the iNaturalist era, and it misses everything before.

## Applied to the suitability

`obis_range_constraint.qmd`) on US waters, unthresholded, so that only the downweight shows. The
lines disagree, so each one is applied in turn. An Alaska ecoregion that a line never searched takes
that line's pooled-Alaska weight: the High Arctic on every line, and every ecoregion except the Gulf

## What this does and does not show

So far, any-turtle weights for Alaska pooled (central, precautionary):

- **Lines with real Alaska effort agree that turtles are rarer there per unit of looking.**
  All records (B) gives `r wv("B. all records", "AK")`, `r wv("B. all records", "AK", "w_prec")`,
  and iNaturalist (C) gives `r wv("C. public reports", "AK")`, `r wv("C. public reports", "AK", "w_prec")`.
  The Gulf of Alaska's share of US turtle suitability falls by one to two orders of magnitude.
- **Folding in the NOAA records (B+) is the generous bound.** It gives `r wv("B+. all records + NOAA AK", "AK")`,
  `r wv("B+. all records + NOAA AK", "AK", "w_prec")`, and the Gulf of Alaska alone sits near half the
  West Coast rate. The truth for the NOAA file is somewhere between B and B+, and line D is the test
  that would place it.
- **OBIS surveys (A) cannot tell yet.** OBIS has `r nr("A. surveys", "AK")` turtle-aware Alaska
  block-years, so the zero there is not yet evidence.

- **The rule.** One multiplicative weight per species × ecoregion, from occupied over searched units,
  relative to the West Coast. Nothing is masked. Critical habitat and the lower 48 are untouched, and
  a single Alaska record cannot flip a cell on or off. The weight shrinks only as fast as the Alaska
  effort justifies.
- **The answer depends on the reporting channel.** Surveys (A) and public reports (C) are
  like-for-like. All records (B) is not, unless the NOAA file is held against an equivalent lower-48
  file, and B+ shows how far that moves things. Line D, strandings normalized by marine mammal
  strandings, is the like-for-like test for the NOAA file and needs the requested counts.
- **The effort definition carries the result, as it did in the regional test.** Seabird-only surveys
  (NPPSD and much of OBIS's Alaska avifauna) are excluded because they never record turtles.
  Counting them would shrink the Alaska weights a great deal without justification.
- **Season is ignored.** Alaska greens are reported mostly October–December and leatherbacks
  July–August (NOAA). An annual weight is the right scale for an annual score, but a seasonal
  product would need the same table by month.
- **Time is ignored on lines B and C**, and line A pools 1950s–2020s. Restricting to 1990 on could be
  checked.
- **Generalising.** The same function gives a weight for every species × ecoregion against that
  species' best-recorded US region, which would make this a candidate rule for the national score.
  If adopted it belongs in msens (`encounter_weight()`) with fixtures: zero detections, the pooled
  fallback, the cap at 1.
